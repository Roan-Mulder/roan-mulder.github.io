Void-Based Platformer Level Design Toolkit

Build Mario Sunshine-style void levels in Unreal Engine 5 with ready-made, beginner-friendly parts.

At a glance

My role
Solo level designer
Time
8 weeks
Engine
Unreal Engine 5.6, Blueprints
Type
School project

What I did

  • Turned research on three platformers into the design of a toolkit with eight ready-made elements.
  • Tested three technical approaches and chose the most beginner-friendly one.
  • Playtested the demo level, changed it in two iterations, and wrote a beginner-friendly How-To guide.

Part 1What it is

The secret levels in Super Mario Sunshine are platforming challenges over an empty space. One missed jump and you fall. I turned the behaviors of those levels into parts that anyone can add to their own meshes (the 3D objects a level is built from) in Unreal Engine 5, with little to no Blueprint work (Blueprints are Unreal's visual scripting: you connect blocks instead of writing code). The Sunshine look is kept for the demo level only.

It's made for three groups: Unreal Engine 5 beginners who want to try level design, fans of 3D platformers who want to recreate secret levels, and advanced users who want to expand an existing toolkit.

The eight elements:

  • Simple movement: moves a platform along an axis.
  • Simple rotation: spins a platform around any axis.
  • Disappearing platform: vanishes and returns on a timer or when stepped on, including alternating patterns.
  • Bouncing platform: a trampoline-style bounce, or a balloon that launches the player in an arc and can be chained.
  • Spline moving platform: follows a drawn path and can pause or rotate at points along it.
  • Void actor: restarts the level when the player falls.
  • End collectable: completes the level.
  • Regular collectable: coins that can be required to unlock the end collectable.

Part 2How I made it

1. Finding the idea

My first proposal was a Banjo-Kazooie character controller. I changed the concept before the first review because the Sunshine secret levels were closer to what a level designer actually does. A review of my first Sunshine draft pushed it further: the preset Sunshine meshes were too narrow, so I switched to behaviors that work on any mesh. In total there were five drafts over three weeks.

2. Research

I researched why these levels work before deciding what to build.

  • I played Sunshine's secret courses, listed which obstacles appear in each, and ranked them from must have to won't have.
  • A pattern analysis of ten games found that most use void levels as optional, higher-difficulty side content.
  • A player experience study compared Sunshine, A Hat in Time and Penny's Big Breakaway, using developer interviews, forum discussions, a playthrough article and reviews. Two findings shaped the toolkit:
    • Sunshine's secret levels were mandatory, and the lead designer later said he regretted that. The other two games made them optional, and players found them less frustrating.
    • Players enjoy these levels because they are pure platforming.
  • A Hat in Time's modding tools give beginners a Time Rift template to start from. That inspired me to build a similar starting point for Unreal Engine 5 beginners.

3. Choosing the technical approach

I tested three ways to build a rotating platform:

  • Timelines (Unreal's built-in animation curves): worked, but weren't modular. I knew them best, so this was a deliberate step out of my comfort zone.
  • Custom actor components (pieces of logic you attach to an object): the logic lives inside the component, so a user drags a mesh into the level, adds the component, and it works. This became the backbone of the toolkit.
  • Target points (markers you place in the level): moved a mesh between two points, but placing the points was too fiddly for a beginner tool.

4. Planning

  • I painted over reference footage to define each core behavior: its direction, rotation axes and states. These became my plan for building each one.
  • I started the Conditions of Satisfaction document (a list of what the finished toolkit has to do) in week 4 and updated it every week.
  • The scope grew from research. A Hat in Time's balloons led to the bouncing platform. [CHECK: alternating platforms became a setting on the disappearing platform, and rotation sequences became rotation points on the spline platform. Is that right?]

5. Building and solving problems

  • Spline rotation: a cube riding the spline turned diagonally on its second rotation. The rotation was blending the long way round. Enabling Shortest Path (a setting that makes it turn the short way) fixed it.
  • Cube fade: on a six-sided cube with six materials, only one face faded. I solved it with a Sequence node (a Blueprint block that runs several steps one after another) and six parallel branches, one per material.
  • Coin system: regular coins link themselves to the end collectable, which counts them and shows a counter. When collection is required, the coins turn red and the end collectable only appears once all are collected.
  • Designed for beginners: settings use dropdowns with working defaults, editor guides show what each setting does, and Blueprint comments are color-coded by purpose.

6. Playtesting

At Play Day (a playtest day at school) my lecturer played the demo level and gave four points: make the blocks colorful, add floors under the harder sections, add collectables, and add annotations. I turned them into two iterations.

  • Iteration 1: short explanations at the resting points, an extra resting point, and the coin system. [CHECK: confirm the order against Miro]
  • Iteration 2: six color materials, a multicolored cube so rotation is easy to read, and floors under the hardest sections, because the demo should be a showcase, not a punishment.

7. Documenting it

I wrote a How-To so a beginner can install the toolkit and build a first level. It has a chapter for each element, a complete level setup guide, a section on adding new content, and troubleshooting. Claude drafted the template and most of the troubleshooting section. I rewrote the draft and verified the entries, and the document credits this at the end.
